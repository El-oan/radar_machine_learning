\section*{Abstract}


In this work, we study and benchmark different deep learning architectures for
Human Activity Recognition (HAR) tasks (e.g., running, jumping, sitting, etc.) using radar data. Point cloud
data offer strong potential due to their 
privacy-preserving and environment-agnostic properties (lighting conditions, etc.). However, point cloud-based HAR
faces challenges such as data sparsity, high computational cost, and a lack of large annotated datasets.

The deep learning architectures we study in this paper are PointNet~\cite{qi2017pointnet}, a skeleton predictor based on DGCNN~\cite{wang2019dgcnn,zhang2026skdgcnn}, methods
using moment characterization, and simpler machine learning classifiers used as baselines. We
benchmarked our models on four datasets, all recorded with mmWave radars and
containing 2 to 10 classes. Our results highlight which architectural choices are most effective
for sparse radar point data and provide practical guidance for future
radar-based human state recognition.

\section{Datasets used}

We studied four radar point datasets: RadHAR, DGUHA,
MARS, and a dataset we recorded ourselves, which we named RaDataTouille.
They all contain different classes and different features, but all of them have a
time dimension, which some architectures use and some do not. Each "image" is called a frame, and 
each frame contains 4 to 40 radar points, which requires a strategy to make that
number fixed. A group of frames is called a window, and windows contain a fixed number of frames.
We aim to keep our windows around 2 seconds long, which is a realistic time for our system
to predict the pose the user is performing in an industrial scenario.

\subsection{RadHAR}
\label{sec:radhar}

The RadHAR dataset, introduced by Singh \textit{et al.}~\cite{singh2019radhar}
at the 3rd ACM Workshop on Millimeter-Wave Networks and Sensing Systems
(mmNets, 2019), is one of the most widely used public benchmarks for human
activity recognition (HAR) from millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar point clouds.
It is also referred to in the literature as the \emph{MMActivity} dataset.

\paragraph{Acquisition setup.}
The data were collected with a Texas Instruments \texttt{IWR1443BOOST} mmWave FMCW
radar, a low-cost, commercial off-the-shelf sensor operating in the
76--81~GHz band. The radar was mounted on a tripod at a height of
$1.3$~m, facing the subject. The sampling rate is approximately $30$~Hz, i.e.\ one frame
every $\approx 33$~ms.

\paragraph{Activities and subjects.}
Two participants performed five activities in front of the radar: boxing,
jumping, jumping jacks, squats, and walking. The dataset is shipped with a
fixed train/test split, provided as separate \texttt{train/} and
\texttt{test/} folders in the official repository. We use this split as-is
throughout the paper, so that all our experiments remain comparable with
prior work using RadHAR. The limited number of subjects (only two) is a
critical caveat, however. Even with a clean train/test separation, the
model can exploit subject-specific cues---gait rhythm, body size,
characteristic arm swing.
Near-ceiling accuracy on RadHAR should therefore be read as performance
on a \emph{closed-world} two-subject task, not as evidence of
generalizable activity recognition.


For every detected point, the following features are provided:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Feature & Description \\
\midrule
$x, y, z$                   & Cartesian coordinates of the point (m) \\
$r$ (\texttt{range})        & Radial distance from the radar (m) \\
$v$ (\texttt{velocity})     & Doppler radial velocity (m/s) \\
$\theta$ (\texttt{bearing}) & Azimuth angle (rad) \\
$I$ (\texttt{intensity})    & Reflected signal intensity \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent These features are not independent: Cartesian coordinates
$(x,y,z)$, range $r$ and bearing $\theta$ are linked by the
spherical-to-Cartesian relation, so the raw representation is
\emph{redundant}. Which subset is fed to a model is therefore a
non-trivial design choice. In practice we found the five-dimensional
representation $(x, y, z, v, I)$ sufficient, discarding the redundant
polar coordinates. The different feature combinations we explored are
detailed in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Effective dataset size.}
After windowing, the 167,632 frames yield approximately 2,793 labeled
samples of 60~frames each. This is the quantity that actually limits
classifier capacity: a model trained on this dataset sees roughly 560
samples per class on average, which is modest by deep-learning standards
and makes per-subject variability a real concern given that only two
subjects contributed data.

\paragraph{Temporal windowing.}
Following the original RadHAR protocol, activities are classified over a
sliding time window of $2$~seconds, which at $30$~fps corresponds to a
sequence of $60$ consecutive frames. Each such window constitutes one
labeled sample for the classifier. The resulting object is therefore a
\emph{sequence of sets}: 60 unordered, variable-size point sets,
collectively carrying a single activity label.


\subsection{RaDataTouille}
\label{sec:radatatouille}

RaDataTouille is the dataset we recorded ourselves with a Texas Instruments
\texttt{xWR68xx AOP} radar operating at 60~GHz. The AOP (Antenna-on-Package)
form factor integrates the antenna array directly on the chip, making the
sensor significantly more compact than the tripod-mounted IWR1443BOOST used
for RadHAR and MARS. This compactness is directly relevant to the project's
target application: a sensor that must be embedded in furniture or worn by
a user cannot use a large benchtop board.

The dataset focuses on a binary posture-recognition problem with two labels:
\texttt{sit} and \texttt{stand~up}. Although this is a simpler classification
problem than the five- or ten-class benchmarks, we recorded the postures in quite difficult environments,
with varied radar angles and spatial configurations (using obstacles, walls, and 
different distances between the radar and the subject, from 50 cm to 3 meters). We recorded
three subjects: Eloan, Othmane and Lucas.

Each detected radar point is represented by six features:
\[
(x, y, z, \mathrm{snr}, \mathrm{velocity}, \mathrm{noise}).
\]
The split contains 13 training records and 6 test records, totaling 19
recordings, 20,031 training frames and 7,380 test frames (27,411 frames
overall). At roughly $15\times$ smaller than RadHAR by frame count, it is a
meaningful stress test of whether the studied architectures generalize under
limited supervision. The recordings are handled at $10$~fps in our tooling, so
a $2$-second window corresponds to $20$ frames.

\subsection{DGUHA}
\label{sec:dguha}

DGUHA, the Dongguk University Human Activity dataset, was introduced by Lee and
Kim in the MTGEA paper~\cite{lee2023mtgea}. It contains synchronized radar
point clouds and Kinect recordings, with seven activity labels: running,
jumping, sitting down and standing up, both upper-limb extension, falling
forward, right-limb extension and left-limb extension.

Each radar point used in this repository carries three features: Cartesian
coordinates $(x,y,z)$.

Each clip consists of 400 frames, and each frame is fixed at 25 points.
Unlike RadHAR's sliding-window protocol, each clip is a complete,
semantically bounded action: the 400-frame window was chosen to span one full
execution of the movement. Since each activity recording lasts approximately
$20$~seconds, DGUHA is about $20$~fps, so a $2$-second window corresponds to
about $40$ frames. The train/test split contains 607 training clips
and 161 test clips (768 total, 307,200 frames). The sensor is a Texas
Instruments \texttt{IWR1443BOOST} mmWave radar---the same board used for
RadHAR and MARS---paired with an Azure Kinect for ground-truth annotations.

\subsection{MARS}
\label{sec:mars}

MARS, for \emph{mmWave-based Assistive Rehabilitation System for Smart
Healthcare}, is a rehabilitation-oriented dataset containing synchronized
mmWave radar data and Kinect skeleton annotations~\cite{an2021mars}. It was collected with a
Texas Instruments
\texttt{IWR1443BOOST} mmWave radar and targets ten rehabilitation movements:
left upper-limb extension, right upper-limb extension, both upper-limb
extension, left front lunge, right front lunge, squat, left side lunge,
right side lunge, left-limb extension and right-limb extension.

Each radar point carries five features:
\[
(x, y, z, \mathrm{Doppler\ velocity}, \mathrm{intensity}),
\]
where $x$ is the lateral axis, $y$ is the depth axis and $z$ is the
vertical axis.

The repository split follows a \emph{subject-based} protocol: recordings
from different participants are assigned exclusively to train or test, so
the evaluation measures genuine inter-subject generalization rather than
interpolation within a single subject's sessions. This is a stricter and
more realistic split than a random frame or window partition. The split
contains 29 training recordings and 10 test recordings, corresponding to
28,221 training frames and 11,801 test frames. The radar frame duration is
$100$~ms ($10$~fps), so a $2$-second window corresponds to $20$ frames.

\paragraph{Note on data leakage in published results.}
The original work associated with MARS reported results using a
\emph{frame-level} random split rather than a subject-based one.
Because consecutive frames from the same recording are highly correlated
--- a window starting at frame $t$ and one at frame $t+1$ share 59 of
their 60 frames --- randomly partitioning at the frame level places
nearly identical windows in both training and test sets. The model
effectively memorizes specific movement executions rather than learning
motion patterns that generalize across subjects. Results obtained under
this split are substantially inflated and are not reproducible under a
subject-isolated evaluation. All our own MARS experiments use the
subject-based split described above.

\section{PointNet}
\label{sec:pointnet}
\sectionauthor{Eloan Tourtelier}

PointNet~\cite{qi2017pointnet} is a fundamental model in point-cloud deep learning.
It was built to process an
unordered set of 3D points with no pixel grid and no voxel ordering. In this project, we use two
variants. V1 is the original vanilla PointNet classifier, which we
validated on the ModelNet40 3D object benchmark from the original PointNet paper~\cite{qi2017pointnet}.
V2 adds explicit temporal processing (over the sequence of frames) using 
an attention mechanism, enabling each point from each frame to "talk" to itself
across the time window.

\subsection{Vanilla PointNet}
\label{sec:vanilla-pointnet}

PointNet processes each radar frame as an unordered point set. The point cloud is first processed 
by a T-Net, which multiplies the distribution by a matrix to rotate/reflect the data.
Each point is then processed independently by a stack of shared MLPs that increase
the dimensionality to $1024$. This process is repeated twice in the original paper, but this can be increased.
A global max-pooling operation then collapses along the point
dimension into a single 1024-dimensional descriptor. Because max-pooling is invariant to
permutation, it is invariant to the order in which points are presented. This permutation
invariance is critical for every model processing radar points,
because detectors return points in
arbitrary order.
Finally, a three-layer MLP head maps the max-pooling output to class logits.
The detailed V1 module layout and hyperparameters are given in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pointnet-v1}.

About the two transformation networks (T-Nets): The input
T-Net predicts a $D \times D$ (the feature dimension) matrix that is applied to the raw point
coordinates, canonicalizing the point cloud orientation before any learned
embedding. Both matrices are regularized in the loss with an orthogonality
penalty ($\lambda = 0.001$) to keep the learned transformations close to a rotation and/or
a reflection, keeping lengths, angles, and volumes unchanged;
without this constraint, the high-dimensional feature T-Net would 
overfit to arbitrary linear maps, while we only want the T-Net to stick to
reorienting the distribution to specialize the layer to this role.
An orthogonal matrix $A$, verifies
\[
A A^\top = I,
\]
and implemented in the loss as a deviation from the identity matrix,
$\lVert I - A A^\top\rVert_F^2$.

    \caption{PointNet architecture. The T-Net modules canonicalize
    point-cloud orientation, while global max pooling produces an

\end{figure}

\subsection{Attention PointNet}
\label{sec:attention-pointnet}

Within a single frame,
points are an unordered set and must be treated symmetrically; across frames,
there is a meaningful ordering, which carries information.
V2 addresses this by stacking a temporal attention mechanism inside the PointNet
encoder, right after the T-Net rotation.

For the attention mechanism, we use multi-head self-attention with 
8 heads or fewer (depending on whether the dimension is large enough for 8 heads). 
Each frame can attend to all others and weight the time
steps most informative for the activity decision. Positional information is
injected via \emph{Rotary Position Encoding} (RoPE), which encodes relative
temporal distances rather than absolute positions between frames. 
We add an attention layer after each T-Net, and therefore have two blocks:
T-Net $\rightarrow$ time attention $\rightarrow$ MLP. The time dimension is mean pooled at the very end,
after max pooling (along the $N$ dimension), and right before the final logits generator.

The addition of the time attention mechanism is not always beneficial.
It seems the longer the time window is (the more frames it contains), the better
the accuracy is. However, for practical reasons, we keep our windows around 2 seconds long,
which is the time one would expect the radar to classify an input in a commercial scenario.
The detailed V2 module layout and hyperparameters are given in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pointnet-v2}.

\subsection{Data Processing}
\label{sec:augmentation}

\paragraph{Normalization.}
Per-feature standardization is applied first: each feature dimension is shifted to zero mean
and rescaled to unit variance using statistics computed on the \emph{training data only}.
The same mean and standard deviation are stored and reapplied at test time, so no
test-distribution information leaks into the preprocessing pipeline. Outliers are then clipped
to be bounded by four times the standard deviation.

\paragraph{Rolling windows.}
Each recording is segmented into overlapping windows using a sliding stride smaller than the
window length. The stride is set to a fraction of the window size (1/2 to 1/3), so
consecutive windows share some of their frames but start at different times in the
cycle. This multiplies the number of labeled samples, but also extracts more information
from the data, as the classifier becomes more agnostic to the beginning of the recording.

\paragraph{Augmentation.} All four datasets share two structural challenges: small subject counts and limited recording
diversity. Data augmentation is therefore essential to prevent models from overfitting to
subject-specific radar signatures. We apply a common augmentation pipeline during training only.
The pipeline operates on raw point clouds before any
architecture-specific preprocessing, and contains scaling, offsetting, and jittering.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{images/training_above_val.png}
    \caption{When adding augmentation to the training process, we observe that the training
    losses are above the validation losses at first, until the model overfits and the training losses
    fall below the validation losses.}
    \label{fig:augmentation-losses}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Scale.}
At each training step, all spatial coordinates $(x, y, z)$ of a window are multiplied by a
scalar drawn uniformly from $[0.85, 1.15]$. This simulates subjects of different heights and
body sizes as well as small calibration offsets in radar mounting distance, reducing the
tendency of models to exploit absolute distance as a class cue.

\paragraph{Offset.}
A random translation vector with each component drawn from
$\mathcal{U}(-0.1\text{ m},\,0.1\text{ m})$ is added to every point in the window.
This encourages position invariance within the sensor field of view and compensates
for the fixed radar placements across datasets.

\paragraph{Point jitter.}
Independent Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.005^2)$ is added to each
coordinate of each point. The amplitude is kept small relative to the unit variance of
the normalized data so that local geometry is preserved while the model becomes robust to
sensor noise and single-frame detection instability.

\paragraph{RadHAR.}
Both PointNet variants perform strongly on RadHAR. V2 averages $96.5\%$
accuracy and $0.9656$ macro F1 across five seeds, slightly above V1, which
averages $95.5\%$ accuracy and $0.9562$ macro F1. The difference is small
relative to the near-ceiling scores, but V2 is more stable on the best run,
reaching $97.7\%$ accuracy and $0.9770$ macro F1 at seed 1237.

\paragraph{DGUHA.}
V2 outperforms V1 by $+0.86$ pp in accuracy and $+0.69$ pp in macro F1 on average.
The gain is consistent across seeds 1234, 1235, and 1237; seed 1236 is a weak run for
both models but V2 still matches V1. The long 400-frame windows in DGUHA give the temporal
attention enough sequence to exploit motion dynamics across the 7 activity classes.

\paragraph{RaDataTouille.}
The picture is reversed: V1 averages $82.6\%$ across all five seeds, while V2 averages
$80.2\%$ (or $82.8\%$ excluding the exploding-gradient run at seed 1236).
The binary sit/stand task is small and the recordings are short ($20$-frame windows), leaving
little temporal structure for the attention mechanism to leverage. In this low-data regime,
the extra parameters of V2 appear to hurt rather than help, consistent with the MARS
observation in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{MARS.}
V1 is also stronger on MARS, averaging $89.0\%$ accuracy and $0.7900$ macro F1,
compared with $84.6\%$ accuracy and $0.7524$ macro F1 for V2. The validation
accuracies are close to perfect for both variants, while test macro F1 remains
lower because both models fail to recognize the left and right limb extension
classes in the subject-isolated test set. This gap suggests that MARS is not
only an optimization problem, but also a generalization problem: the models fit
the training subjects well, yet struggle when the same rehabilitation gestures
are performed by an unseen subject.

\section{Sketching}
\label{sec:sketching-transformer}
\sectionauthor{Othmane Ouali}

\subsection{Preprocessing: Random Fourier Feature sketching}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

\paragraph{Why preprocessing is needed.}
A raw radar frame is an \emph{unordered set of points of variable
cardinality}. No standard learning architecture --- be it an MLP, a CNN,
a Transformer or a gradient-boosted tree --- can consume such an object
directly: they all expect inputs of fixed shape and well-defined
structure. A preprocessing step is therefore unavoidable. The original
RadHAR baseline addresses this issue through \emph{voxelization}: each
2-second window is discretized into a tensor of shape
$60 \times 10 \times 32 \times 32 = 614\,400$ scalar
features~\cite{singh2019radhar}. This representation is extremely
high-dimensional and overwhelmingly sparse, since a typical frame
contains only a few tens of points scattered in a grid of $10{,}240$
voxels. The downstream model thus has to invest most of its capacity in
processing empty space.

We propose instead to compress each frame into a compact, fixed-size
vector via a \emph{random Fourier feature map}, which preserves the
information carried by the points while keeping the dimensionality
manageable.

\paragraph{Random Fourier feature sketching.}
For a given choice of $d$ per-point features
(see paragraph \emph{Feature selection} below), a frame at time $t$ is
a point set $\mathcal{F}_t = \{p_1, \ldots, p_{N_t}\} \subset
\mathbb{R}^d$, where $N_t$ varies from frame to frame. We map this set
to a fixed-size sketch $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$ in two steps.

\emph{Step 1 --- Per-point random projection.} We draw a single random
matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ with i.i.d.\ entries
\[
W_{ij} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
\]
\emph{once and for all} at the beginning of training, and freeze it.
Each point $p_i$ is mapped to
\[
\varphi(p_i) \;=\; \sin(W p_i) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{m}.
\]
This is a Fourier-type random feature map (with bias $b = 0$ and using
the sine component only), inspired by the random Fourier feature
construction of Rahimi and Recht~\cite{rahimi2007random}. With
$b = 0$ and a single trigonometric component, the inner product
$\langle \varphi(p_i), \varphi(p_j)\rangle$ does not strictly
approximate a Gaussian kernel; we use it nonetheless as a randomized,
non-linear feature map, which proved sufficient for our task.

\emph{Step 2 --- Aggregation across the frame.} Since
$\mathcal{F}_t$ is a \emph{set}, the aggregation across its points must
be permutation-invariant. We consider two variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform sketch:}
    \(\displaystyle s_t \;=\; \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \sin(W p_i).\)
    \item \textbf{Intensity-weighted sketch:}
    \(\displaystyle s_t \;=\; \frac{1}{\sum_i I_i}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} I_i\,\sin(W p_i),\)
\end{itemize}
where $I_i$ denotes the reflected intensity of point $i$. The
intensity-weighted variant gives more importance to high-confidence
reflections, which in our experiments proved beneficial.

The whole window is then represented by a tensor of shape
$60 \times m$, obtained by stacking the 60 per-frame sketches.

\paragraph{Feature standardization.}
Per-point features live on widely different scales: range is in metres
($r \in [0, 10]$ approximately), bearing in radians
($\theta \in [-\pi, \pi]$), velocity in m/s, intensity on an arbitrary
scale. Because the random projection $W p_i$ mixes all features
linearly, this scale heterogeneity would make the choice of $\sigma$
ill-defined. We therefore standardize every feature to zero mean and
unit variance \emph{before} applying the sketching step. The
per-feature mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $s$ are computed
\emph{on the training set only}, and the same statistics are reused at
test time, so that no information leaks from the test set into the
preprocessing pipeline.

\paragraph{Feature selection.}
The seven raw features per point ($x, y, z, r, v, \theta, I$) are
redundant: $(x, y, z)$, $r$ and $\theta$ are linked by the
spherical-to-Cartesian relation. Determining which subset is most
informative is therefore an empirical question. We compared four
configurations:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Configuration       & Per-point features           & Aggregation \\
\midrule
All features        & $(x, y, z, r, v, \theta, I)$ & uniform \\
Cartesian + I       & $(x, y, z, I)$               & uniform \\
Polar + I           & $(\theta, r, I)$             & uniform \\
Polar + I (weighted)& $(\theta, r, I)$             & $I$-weighted \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The polar configuration $(\theta, r, I)$ consistently
outperformed the Cartesian variants, which motivated the introduction
of the intensity-weighted sketch as a refinement of that
configuration. A detailed comparison is reported in the ablation
study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Sliding-window construction.}
Each raw recording is segmented into 2-second windows (60 consecutive
frames) using a sliding window with a stride of $10$ frames. This
yields a controlled overlap (50 frames, $\approx 83\%$) between
consecutive windows: small enough to keep them distinct from the
classifier's point of view, large enough to make full use of the
available recordings. The procedure produces a total of $12\,150$
training windows and $3\,565$ test windows, in line with the official
RadHAR train/test split.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter choices.}
The preprocessing pipeline introduces several hyperparameters whose
impact is non-trivial: the random-projection scale $\sigma$, the
sketch dimension $m$, the sliding-window stride, the per-point
feature subset, and the aggregation scheme (uniform vs.\
intensity-weighted). All of them were studied empirically; the
corresponding ablation study is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. Unless stated otherwise, the rest of the
paper uses $\sigma = 1$, $m = 256$, polar features
$(\theta, r, I)$, intensity-weighted aggregation, and a stride of
$10$ frames.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The full preprocessing pipeline can be summarized as
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{recording} &\rightarrow \text{frames}
\rightarrow \text{2-s windows} \\
&\rightarrow \text{sketched windows}
\rightarrow \text{model input.}
\end{aligned}
\]
The data finally fed to all models compared in this paper is therefore
a tensor of shape $(12\,150, 60, 256)$ for training and
$(3\,565, 60, 256)$ for testing.


\subsection{Transformer encoder}
\label{sec:transformer}

\paragraph{Overview.}
The preprocessing stage turns each 2-second window into a sequence of
$60$ sketches $s_1, \ldots, s_{60} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$, one per frame.
We treat this sequence as a sequence of $60$ tokens and process it with
a Transformer encoder, which models temporal dependencies between frames
through self-attention. We deliberately do not introduce an input projection
layer: the sketches are fed directly to the encoder, so that the model
dimension equals the sketch dimension, $d_{\text{model}} = m$. As a side
effect, varying $m$ in the ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) also
varies the size of the Transformer.

\paragraph{Positional encoding.}
Self-attention is permutation-invariant: it would treat the $60$ frames as an
unordered set and discard the temporal order that distinguishes activities such
as walking from boxing. To restore the temporal order, we add a fixed
sinusoidal positional encoding~\cite{vaswani2017attention} to each sketch before
the first encoder layer:
\[
\tilde{s}_t = s_t + \mathrm{PE}(t), \qquad t = 1, \ldots, 60.
\]
For each position $t$ and each dimension $i = 0, \ldots, m-1$:
\[
\mathrm{PE}(t)_{2i} = \sin\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i / m}}\right),
\quad
\mathrm{PE}(t)_{2i+1} = \cos\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i / m}}\right).
\]
This encoding is computed once and kept fixed throughout training.

\paragraph{Encoder block.}
Each encoder layer follows the standard
Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} design: a multi-head self-attention
sublayer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, each wrapped in a
residual connection and layer normalization (post-norm). The self-attention uses $h = 4$
heads; the feed-forward sublayer has a single hidden layer of width $2m$ with
a GELU activation. Dropout with rate $p = 0.4$ is applied inside each sublayer.
We stack $L = 3$ such layers.

\paragraph{Classification head.}
After the encoder layers, we obtain a sequence of contextualized frame
representations $h_1, \ldots, h_{60}$. We aggregate them by mean pooling over
the temporal axis,
\[
\bar{h} = \frac{1}{60}\sum_{t=1}^{60} h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m},
\]
and feed the result to a linear layer that outputs the activity logits. We use
mean pooling rather than a dedicated \texttt{[CLS]} token because it gave
comparable results without introducing an extra learned token.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.}
The architecture is governed by four hyperparameters: the model dimension
$m = 256$, the number of encoder layers $L = 3$, the number of attention heads
$h = 4$, and the dropout rate $p = 0.4$.

The full architecture is summarized in
Figure~\ref{fig:transformer-architecture} (Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}).


\subsection{Training protocol}
\label{sec:training}

\paragraph{Loss and optimization.}
We minimize the cross-entropy loss between predicted logits and ground-truth
activity labels. Optimization is performed with Adam at a fixed learning rate
of $10^{-4}$, with no weight decay and no learning-rate schedule. We use a
mini-batch size of $32$ and train for $30$ epochs, without early stopping.

\paragraph{Weight initialization.}
All trainable weight matrices in the Transformer encoder and final
classification head are initialized with Kaiming initialization; biases are
initialized to zero. The random-projection matrix $W$ of the sketching step is
not trained: it is drawn once from $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ at the start of
training and kept fixed.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
We use the official RadHAR train/test split ($12\,150$ training windows and
$3\,565$ test windows) without carving out a separate validation set. The final
test accuracy reported in the main comparison corresponds to the model state at
the last training epoch.

\paragraph{Training dynamics.}
The training loss decreases smoothly toward zero, while the test loss decreases
overall but exhibits large epoch-to-epoch variance. This pattern is not a
typical sign of overfitting, where the test loss would increase monotonically;
we attribute it primarily to a distributional gap between the training and test
splits of RadHAR.

\section{Pointwise Attention Pooling}
\label{sec:pointwise-attention-pooling}
\sectionauthor{Lucas Cheylan}

This section presents the raw-point attention model studied as a direct
classifier for radar activity recognition. It is designed for sparse radar
sequences, where each recording is a temporal window of frames and each frame
contains an unordered set of radar detections.

\subsection{Input representation}

For every dataset, the ingestion scripts convert the raw files into the same
working format: a sequence of radar frames. Each frame contains points described
by their position, velocity and radar response,
\[
    p_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i, v_i, I_i).
\]
The number of detections varies from one frame to another. To obtain fixed-size
tensors, we keep at most 32 points per frame and pad shorter frames with empty
points. A binary mask is carried through the model so that padded entries do
not contribute to the attention weights or to the pooled descriptors.

Training samples are built from windows of 60 consecutive frames with stride
10. The resulting input tensor has shape
\[
    X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times P \times F},
\]
where $T=60$ is the number of frames, $P=32$ is the maximum number of points per
frame, and $F=5$ is the number of point features.

\subsection{Model mechanism}

The architecture is built around two learned pooling operations. First, a
shared pointwise MLP embeds every radar detection independently. Since the same
MLP is applied to all points, the model does not rely on an arbitrary ordering
inside the point cloud. Then, an intra-frame attention pooling layer learns
which detections are useful in each frame. Noisy or padded points can receive a
small weight, while points associated with the body or with visible motion can
receive a larger one.

After this first pooling stage, every frame is represented by one vector
$z_t$. The sequence $(z_1,\ldots,z_T)$ is passed to a bidirectional GRU, which
captures the temporal evolution of the activity. A second attention pooling
layer is then applied over the GRU outputs. This gives a single sequence
descriptor
\[
    s = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_t h_t,
\]
where $h_t$ is the GRU state at time $t$ and $\alpha_t$ is the learned temporal
attention weight. The final activity class is predicted from $s$ with a small
classifier head.

The core idea is therefore to learn two levels of importance: which radar
points are informative inside each frame, and which frames are informative
inside the temporal window. This makes the model a middle ground between
hand-crafted summaries and heavier point-cloud architectures. It still operates
on raw detections, but it keeps the pooling operations explicit and relatively
lightweight.

\subsection{Experimental protocol}

The same reference pipeline was run on four cleaned datasets using their native
train/test splits. Although the raw formats differ across datasets, the
preprocessing step maps all of them to the same frame-based representation with
points $(x,y,z,v,I)$. The evaluation protocol is then shared: windows of 60
frames, stride 10, up to 32 points per frame, and 12 training epochs.

\section{Skeleton classifier with dgcnn}
\label{sec:skeleton-classifier}
\sectionauthor{Eloan Tourtelier}

The SK-DGCNN approach follows a sequential two-stage strategy adapted from the
SK-DGCNN method~\cite{zhang2026skdgcnn}:
instead of classifying sparse radar points directly, it first maps each radar
frame to a structured human skeleton and then classifies the resulting skeleton. 
This approach has been described as successful in many cases, and
it gives the model more information in principle, especially about skeleton posture feasibility:
for example, a foot cannot be 1 meter away from the knee. It also enables the use
of external datasets, counterbalancing data sparsity issues for the target dataset.

\subsection{Radar-to-skeleton stage}

The first stage is a skeleton-aware DGCNN regressor trained on MARS~\cite{an2021mars}. DGCNN is a
very strong model, built as a generalization of PointNet~\cite{qi2017pointnet} by introducing
spatial neighborhoods using graphs~\cite{wang2019dgcnn}. In MARS, radar frames are synchronized with Kinect
skeleton annotations, which record the real skeleton positions.
A dynamic $k$-nearest-neighbor graph
is recomputed inside the network, and three EdgeConv blocks extract local
geometric features. Efficient channel attention is added after each block so
that the network can reweight the most informative channels before global
pooling.

The output is a centered 19-joint skeleton using the lateral and vertical
coordinates $(x,z)$ (according to the original paper, this is the best configuration, better than 
the 3D representation), giving 38 regression targets per frame. We center the
skeleton around the \texttt{SpineShoulder} joint so that the regressor focuses
on body pose rather than absolute position in the radar field of view.
The pose regressor follows the SK-DGCNN paper's shape-aware objective: a
coordinate-wise MSE term keeps individual joints close to their Kinect target,
while a KL-divergence term treats the predicted and target skeleton coordinates
as spatial distributions and penalizes global shape mismatch. For one active
axis $c \in \{x,z\}$ with predicted coordinates $\hat{s}_{i,c}$ and target
coordinates $s_{i,c}$ over $J=19$ joints,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE},c}
= \frac{1}{J}\sum_{i=1}^{J}(\hat{s}_{i,c}-s_{i,c})^2,
\]
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL},c}
&= \sum_{i=1}^{J} q_{i,c}\log\frac{q_{i,c}}{p_{i,c}},\\
p_c &= \mathrm{softmax}(\hat{s}_{:,c}),\\
q_c &= \mathrm{softmax}(s_{:,c}).
\end{aligned}
\]
The mixed loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{c}
= \alpha_{\mathrm{MSE}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE},c}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{KL}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL},c},
\]
with dynamic weights
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.05\left\lfloor\frac{\mathrm{epoch}}{20}\right\rfloor,
\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{MSE}} = 1-\alpha_{\mathrm{KL}}.
\]
In our two-axis setup, the total loss sums the active axes,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{L}_c$, where
$\mathcal{A}$ grows from $z$ to $(z,x)$ during sequential multitask training.
The architecture and training hyperparameters for this stage are summarized in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:appendix-skdgcnn}. This regression part is the hardest in the sequence
and requires very compute-intensive training (200 epochs).

\subsection{Skeleton classification stage}

Once the radar-to-skeleton checkpoint is trained, it can be used to transform
radar recordings into skeleton sequences. Dataset windows are converted from raw radar point
clouds into predicted 19-joint skeletons. The activity classifier then operates
on this structured sequence rather than on the original point cloud. This
separates the perception problem from the classification problem: the first
network learns a radar-to-body representation, and the second network learns
activity dynamics from body motion.

This design is expected to help when the activity is better explained by joint
configuration than by the raw distribution of radar reflections. Its main
drawback is error propagation: if the skeleton regressor fails on a frame, the
classifier only sees the incorrect skeleton and cannot recover the discarded
radar evidence.


\subsection{Data Processing}
\label{sec:skeleton-data-processing}

\paragraph{Radar-to-skeleton preprocessing.}
For the pose-estimation stage, each MARS radar frame is matched with its
synchronized Kinect annotation. Radar frames are cropped or padded to 25 points,
using the five MARS radar features $x,y,z$, Doppler velocity and intensity. The
Kinect target is reduced to the 19-joint contract used by SK-DGCNN and to the
two coordinates $(x,z)$, corresponding to lateral and vertical motion. The
Kinect lateral axis is inverted to align it with the radar coordinate frame, and
the skeleton is centered on \texttt{SpineShoulder}.

\paragraph{Skeleton cache generation.}
After training the pose regressor on MARS, the checkpoint is applied to the other datasets
to turn them into skeletons. Each radar frame is converted into a
predicted 19-joint skeleton. The resulting representation keeps only body
motion and discards the original radar points, producing sequences of centered
lateral and vertical joint coordinates for classification.

\paragraph{Windowing and normalization.}
The classifier uses fixed-length skeleton windows from the cached sequences.
Before classification, every skeleton is centered again around
\texttt{SpineShoulder}. Training-set statistics are then used to standardize the
lateral and vertical coordinates, and the same statistics are reused at
validation and test time. A constant confidence channel is concatenated to the
two coordinate channels, so the ST-GCN++ input has three channels over time and
over the 19-joint skeleton graph.

\paragraph{Augmentation.}
During classifier training only, small Gaussian jitter is added to the centered
skeleton coordinates. The skeleton is re-centered after jittering so that the
augmentation changes pose and local motion slightly without shifting the whole
body in the coordinate frame.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:skeleton-results}

The skeleton pipeline is evaluated in two parts: first as a radar-to-skeleton
regressor on MARS, then as a full skeleton-based activity classifier on the respective datasets.
Central torso joints are reconstructed most accurately, while wrists and
feet have larger errors because they move farther from the body center and are
harder to infer from sparse radar reflections. 
For the skeleton regressor, mean absolute error is computed over the two
predicted coordinates $(z,x)$:
\[
\mathrm{MAE}
= \frac{1}{2NJ}
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\sum_{c\in\{z,x\}}
\left|\hat{s}^{(n)}_{i,c}-s^{(n)}_{i,c}\right|,
\]
where $N$ is the number of frames and $J=19$ is the number of joints.

\section{Moments}
\label{sec:moments}
\sectionauthor{Meriem Mellouki and Zaineb Tarhda}

\subsection{Motivation}

The sketching approach described in Section~\ref{sec:sketching-transformer}
compresses each frame into a fixed-size vector via a random Fourier feature map.
A conceptually simpler alternative is to summarize the empirical distribution of
points in a frame by its \emph{statistical moments}: mean, variance, skewness,
kurtosis, and range. This representation is fully deterministic, requires no
learned or random parameters, and is permutation-invariant by construction.

While statistical moments cannot, in general, uniquely characterize an arbitrary
distribution, a classical result in probability theory guarantees that the first
$2N$ moments uniquely determine any discrete distribution supported on at most
$N$ points~\cite{akhiezer1965classical}. Since each radar frame typically
contains fewer than 30 points, a moderate number of moments is therefore
\emph{sufficient to characterize the frame exactly}, up to point-set
permutation. This property makes the moment representation particularly
well-suited to the sparse radar setting, where voxel-based or
Fourier-based approaches would introduce unnecessary complexity.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:moments-preprocessing}

\paragraph{Feature selection.}
Each detected point carries seven raw features: $x, y, z$ (Cartesian
coordinates), range $r$, radial velocity $v$, bearing $\theta$, and intensity
$I$. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:radhar}, Cartesian coordinates and polar
features are geometrically redundant. We found it sufficient to work with a
compact five-dimensional per-point representation:
\[
p_i = (x_i,\; y_i,\; z_i,\; v_i,\; I_i) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^5.
\]

\paragraph{Moment extraction.}
For a frame $\mathcal{F}_t = \{p_1, \ldots, p_{N_t}\}$, we compute six
statistics along each of the $d = 5$ feature dimensions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Statistic & Definition \\
\midrule
Mean          & $\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i p_{ik}$ \\[4pt]
Std.\ dev.    & $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i (p_{ik}-\mu_k)^2}$ \\[4pt]
Skewness      & $\gamma_{1,k} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i \left(\frac{p_{ik}-\mu_k}{\sigma_k}\right)^3$ \\[4pt]
Kurtosis      & $\gamma_{2,k} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i \left(\frac{p_{ik}-\mu_k}{\sigma_k}\right)^4 - 3$ \\[4pt]
Minimum       & $\min_i\, p_{ik}$ \\[4pt]
Maximum       & $\max_i\, p_{ik}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Concatenating these statistics across all $d = 5$ dimensions yields a
fixed-size descriptor
\[
s_t \;=\; \bigl[\mu_1,\,\sigma_1,\,\gamma_{1,1},\,\gamma_{2,1},\,
               \min_1,\,\max_1,\;\ldots\;\bigr] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{30},
\]
regardless of the number of points $N_t$ in the frame. This descriptor serves
as the input to the classifier described below.

\paragraph{Temporal aggregation.}
In contrast to the sketching pipeline, which operates over a 2-second window of
60 frames and feeds a $(60, m)$ tensor to a sequential model, the moment
baseline treats each frame \emph{independently}: a single frame is compressed
into one 30-dimensional vector, which is passed directly to an MLP. This
amounts to ignoring the temporal structure of the recording. The implications
for performance and a discussion of how temporal information could be
reintroduced are given in Section~\ref{sec:moments-discussion}.

\paragraph{Standardization.}
The 30-dimensional feature vector is standardized to zero mean and unit
variance using statistics computed on the training set only, following the
same protocol as in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\subsection{Architecture: Multi-Layer Perceptron}

Since the 30-dimensional moment vector has no spatial or sequential structure,
convolutional or recurrent architectures bring no benefit here. We therefore use
a fully-connected MLP with three hidden layers:

\centering
\scriptsize
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Layer   & Output dim & Activation & Note \\
\midrule
Input   & 30         & ---        & Standardized moment vector \\
Dense 1 & 128        & ReLU       & \\
Dense 2 & 64         & ReLU       & \\
Dense 3 & 32         & ReLU       & \\
Output  & 5          & Softmax    & One logit per activity class \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
}
\caption{MLP architecture used for the moment-based baseline.}
\label{tab:moments-mlp}

The model is trained with early stopping (patience of 10 epochs,
validation fraction 15\%) to prevent overfitting on the relatively small

severely regularized by the low input dimensionality.

vertical motion confined to a small spatial region (\textit{squats},
\textit{jack}) are poorly recalled (20\%), likely because a single-frame
moment descriptor cannot encode the periodicity that distinguishes these
activities from a static standing posture.

This limitation is a direct consequence of the \emph{temporal blindness} of
the current approach. Integrating temporal information --- for instance by
computing moments over a 2-second sliding window rather than per frame, or by
feeding the sequence of per-frame descriptors to an LSTM --- is the most
natural next step and is expected to close a significant part of the gap with
the sequential architectures described in
Section~\ref{sec:sketching-transformer}.

\subsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{RadHAR near-ceiling performance and its caveat.}
PointNet V1 and V2 both reach near-ceiling performance on RadHAR, with V2
averaging $96.5\%$ accuracy and V1 averaging $95.5\%$.
These numbers look strong but should be interpreted carefully: RadHAR was
recorded by only two subjects, so a classifier can achieve high accuracy
by recognizing body-shape or gait signatures that are specific to those
individuals rather than by learning the activity itself. The practical
implication is that RadHAR accuracy figures are systematically optimistic
relative to what one would expect on an unseen subject. The two-subject
limitation is a ceiling on how much RadHAR results can say about
generalization.

\paragraph{Temporal attention provides no lift on RadHAR.}
V2 improves over V1 by about $1.0$ percentage point in accuracy
($96.49\%$ vs.\ $95.48\%$) and $0.0094$ macro F1
($0.9656$ vs.\ $0.9562$) on RadHAR. This gain is modest compared with the
overall level of performance, which suggests that the temporal attention
mechanism helps slightly but is not the main bottleneck. For activities like
walking or boxing, a single-frame point cloud already carries a distinctive
spatial signature, so modeling how the cloud evolves over time provides
diminishing returns on a saturated problem.

\paragraph{Vanilla PointNet outperforms the temporal variant on MARS.}
On the harder, subject-isolated MARS benchmark, V1 (89.0\%, macro F1 0.790)
actually outperforms V2 (84.6\%, macro F1 0.752). This reversal is
counterintuitive and suggests a training-efficiency issue: V2's additional
attention parameters require more data to optimize reliably, and the MARS
training set (3 subjects, 383 windows) may simply not be large enough to
take advantage of temporal modeling. It also highlights that architectural
complexity is not always beneficial in low-data regimes.

\paragraph{PointNet V2 helps on DGUHA.}
On DGUHA (7 classes), PointNet V2 averages 92.8\% accuracy across five seeds,
compared with 91.9\% for PointNet V1. The temporal attention layer therefore
provides a modest gain on this longer-window dataset, where motion dynamics are
more informative than in shorter binary settings.

\paragraph{The Moments MLP gap quantifies the value of temporal context.}
The moment-based MLP reaches only 58.6\% on RadHAR, a gap of $\sim\!38$
percentage points below PointNet V1. Since the MLP is temporally blind
(it classifies one frame at a time), this gap directly quantifies how much
information is carried by the temporal dynamics of the point cloud.
Activities that are spatially ambiguous in a single frame---squats and
jumping jacks are both vertical, periodic motions with a small spatial
footprint---require temporal context to be disambiguable. This is the
single clearest argument in the results for using sequence-aware
architectures rather than frame-independent baselines.
