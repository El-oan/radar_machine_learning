\begin{abstract}
We study posture classification from radar point clouds and compare several model
architectures for distinguishing between sitting and standing positions. We describe our radar point representation, training protocol, and evaluation setting, and we benchmark multiple baselines under the same experimental conditions. Our results highlight which architectural choices are most effective for sparse radar point data and provide practical guidance for future radar-based human state recognition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data}

\subsection{RadHAR dataset}
\label{sec:radhar}

The RadHAR dataset, introduced by Singh \textit{et al.}~\cite{singh2019radhar}
at the 3rd ACM Workshop on Millimeter-Wave Networks and Sensing Systems
(mmNets, 2019), is one of the most widely used public benchmarks for human
activity recognition (HAR) from millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar point clouds.
It is also referred to in the literature as the \emph{MMActivity} dataset.

\paragraph{Acquisition setup.}
Data was collected with a Texas Instruments \texttt{IWR1443BOOST} mmWave FMCW
radar, a low-cost, commercial off-the-shelf sensor operating in the
76--81~GHz band. The radar was mounted on a tripod at a height of
$1.3$~m, facing the subject. The on-board signal-processing pipeline
(through the \texttt{radar-lab} ROS package for TI mmWave devices)
converts the raw intermediate-frequency signal into a stream of sparse 3D
point clouds at a sampling rate of approximately $30$~Hz, i.e.\ one frame
every $\approx 33$~ms.

\paragraph{Activities and subjects.}
Two participants performed five activities in front of the radar: boxing,
jumping, jumping jacks, squats and walking. The dataset is shipped with a
fixed train/test split, provided as separate \texttt{train/} and
\texttt{test/} folders in the official repository. We use this split as-is
throughout the paper, so that all our experiments remain comparable with
prior work using RadHAR. We note however that the limited number of
subjects (only two) is an important caveat: it limits the inter-subject
variability seen at training time, and tends to inflate test accuracies
compared to what one would expect on a more diverse population.

\paragraph{Structure of a recording.}
Each recording is a time-ordered sequence of \emph{radar frames}. Two
properties make this representation fundamentally different from image or
video data and condition every design choice that follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sparsity and variable cardinality.} Each frame contains
    only a small, \emph{variable} number of detected points (typically a
    few tens), since a low-cost mmWave radar produces sparse and
    non-uniform point clouds.
    \item \textbf{Unordered point sets.} Within a frame, points have no
    natural ordering: a frame is a \emph{set}, not a sequence. Any
    architecture operating directly on raw points should therefore be
    permutation-invariant at the frame level.
\end{itemize}

For every detected point, six features are provided:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Feature & Description \\
\midrule
$x, y, z$                   & Cartesian coordinates of the point (m) \\
$r$ (\texttt{range})        & Radial distance from the radar (m) \\
$v$ (\texttt{velocity})     & Doppler radial velocity (m/s) \\
$\theta$ (\texttt{bearing}) & Azimuth angle (rad) \\
$I$ (\texttt{intensity})    & Reflected signal intensity \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The six features are not independent: Cartesian coordinates
$(x,y,z)$, range $r$ and bearing $\theta$ are linked by the
spherical-to-Cartesian relation, so the raw representation is
\emph{redundant}. Which subset of features is actually fed to a model is
therefore a non-trivial design choice. The different combinations we
explored, and the rationale behind each, are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Temporal windowing.}
Following the original RadHAR protocol, activities are classified over a
sliding time window of $2$~seconds, which at $30$~fps corresponds to a
sequence of $60$ consecutive frames. Each such window constitutes one
labeled sample for the classifier. The resulting object is therefore a
\emph{sequence of sets}: 60 unordered, variable-size point sets,
collectively carrying a single activity label.

\section{Sketching + Transformer}
\label{sec:sketching-transformer}

\subsection{Preprocessing: Random Fourier Feature sketching}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

\paragraph{Why preprocessing is needed.}
A raw radar frame is an \emph{unordered set of points of variable
cardinality}. No standard learning architecture --- be it an MLP, a CNN,
a Transformer or a gradient-boosted tree --- can consume such an object
directly: they all expect inputs of fixed shape and well-defined
structure. A preprocessing step is therefore unavoidable. The original
RadHAR baseline addresses this issue through \emph{voxelization}: each
2-second window is discretized into a tensor of shape
$60 \times 10 \times 32 \times 32 = 614\,400$ scalar
features~\cite{singh2019radhar}. This representation is extremely
high-dimensional and overwhelmingly sparse, since a typical frame
contains only a few tens of points scattered in a grid of $10{,}240$
voxels. The downstream model thus has to invest most of its capacity in
processing empty space.

We propose instead to compress each frame into a compact, fixed-size
vector via a \emph{random Fourier feature map}, which preserves the
information carried by the points while keeping the dimensionality
manageable.

\paragraph{Random Fourier feature sketching.}
For a given choice of $d$ per-point features
(see paragraph \emph{Feature selection} below), a frame at time $t$ is
a point set $\mathcal{F}_t = \{p_1, \ldots, p_{N_t}\} \subset
\mathbb{R}^d$, where $N_t$ varies from frame to frame. We map this set
to a fixed-size sketch $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$ in two steps.

\emph{Step 1 --- Per-point random projection.} We draw a single random
matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ with i.i.d.\ entries
\[
W_{ij} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),
\]
\emph{once and for all} at the beginning of training, and freeze it.
Each point $p_i$ is mapped to
\[
\varphi(p_i) \;=\; \sin(W p_i) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{m}.
\]
This is a Fourier-type random feature map (with bias $b = 0$ and using
the sine component only), inspired by the random Fourier feature
construction of Rahimi and Recht~\cite{rahimi2007random}. With
$b = 0$ and a single trigonometric component, the inner product
$\langle \varphi(p_i), \varphi(p_j)\rangle$ does not strictly
approximate a Gaussian kernel; we use it nonetheless as a randomized,
non-linear feature map, which proved sufficient for our task.

\emph{Step 2 --- Aggregation across the frame.} Since
$\mathcal{F}_t$ is a \emph{set}, the aggregation across its points must
be permutation-invariant. We consider two variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform sketch:}
    \(\displaystyle s_t \;=\; \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \sin(W p_i).\)
    \item \textbf{Intensity-weighted sketch:}
    \(\displaystyle s_t \;=\; \frac{1}{\sum_i I_i}\sum_{i=1}^{N_t} I_i\,\sin(W p_i),\)
\end{itemize}
where $I_i$ denotes the reflected intensity of point $i$. The
intensity-weighted variant gives more importance to high-confidence
reflections, which in our experiments proved beneficial.

The whole window is then represented by a tensor of shape
$60 \times m$, obtained by stacking the 60 per-frame sketches.

\paragraph{Feature standardization.}
Per-point features live on widely different scales: range is in metres
($r \in [0, 10]$ approximately), bearing in radians
($\theta \in [-\pi, \pi]$), velocity in m/s, intensity on an arbitrary
scale. Because the random projection $W p_i$ mixes all features
linearly, this scale heterogeneity would make the choice of $\sigma$
ill-defined. We therefore standardize every feature to zero mean and
unit variance \emph{before} applying the sketching step. The
per-feature mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $s$ are computed
\emph{on the training set only}, and the same statistics are reused at
test time, so that no information leaks from the test set into the
preprocessing pipeline.

\paragraph{Feature selection.}
The six raw features per point ($x, y, z, r, v, \theta, I$) are
redundant: $(x, y, z)$, $r$ and $\theta$ are linked by the
spherical-to-Cartesian relation. Determining which subset is most
informative is therefore an empirical question. We compared four
configurations:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Configuration       & Per-point features           & Aggregation \\
\midrule
All features        & $(x, y, z, r, v, \theta, I)$ & uniform \\
Cartesian + I       & $(x, y, z, I)$               & uniform \\
Polar + I           & $(\theta, r, I)$             & uniform \\
Polar + I (weighted)& $(\theta, r, I)$             & $I$-weighted \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The polar configuration $(\theta, r, I)$ consistently
outperformed the Cartesian variants, which motivated the introduction
of the intensity-weighted sketch as a refinement of that
configuration. A detailed comparison is reported in the ablation
study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\paragraph{Sliding-window construction.}
Each raw recording is segmented into 2-second windows (60 consecutive
frames) using a sliding window with a stride of $10$ frames. This
yields a controlled overlap (50 frames, $\approx 83\%$) between
consecutive windows: small enough to keep them distinct from the
classifier's point of view, large enough to make full use of the
available recordings. The procedure produces a total of $12\,150$
training windows and $3\,565$ test windows, in line with the official
RadHAR train/test split.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter choices.}
The preprocessing pipeline introduces several hyperparameters whose
impact is non-trivial: the random-projection scale $\sigma$, the
sketch dimension $m$, the sliding-window stride, the per-point
feature subset, and the aggregation scheme (uniform vs.\
intensity-weighted). All of them were studied empirically; the
corresponding ablation study is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. Unless stated otherwise, the rest of the
paper uses $\sigma = 1$, $m = 256$, polar features
$(\theta, r, I)$, intensity-weighted aggregation, and a stride of
$10$ frames.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The full preprocessing pipeline can be summarized as
\[
\underbrace{\text{recording}}_{\text{raw \texttt{.txt}}}
\;\rightarrow\;
\underbrace{\text{frames}}_{\text{sets, var.\ size}}
\;\rightarrow\;
\underbrace{\text{2-s windows}}_{60 \text{ frames}}
\;\rightarrow\;
\underbrace{\text{sketched windows}}_{(60, m)}
\;\rightarrow\;
\text{model input.}
\]
The data finally fed to all models compared in this paper is therefore
a tensor of shape $(12\,150, 60, 256)$ for training and
$(3\,565, 60, 256)$ for testing.

\section{Moments}
\label{sec:moments}

\subsection{Motivation}

The sketching approach described in Section~\ref{sec:sketching-transformer}
compresses each frame into a fixed-size vector via a random Fourier feature map.
A conceptually simpler alternative is to summarize the empirical distribution of
points in a frame by its \emph{statistical moments}: mean, variance, skewness,
kurtosis, and range. This representation is fully deterministic, requires no
learned or random parameters, and is permutation-invariant by construction.

While statistical moments cannot, in general, uniquely characterize an arbitrary
distribution, a classical result in probability theory guarantees that the first
$2N$ moments uniquely determine any discrete distribution supported on at most
$N$ points~\cite{akhiezer1965classical}. Since each radar frame typically
contains fewer than 30 points, a moderate number of moments is therefore
\emph{sufficient to characterize the frame exactly}, up to point-set
permutation. This property makes the moment representation particularly
well-suited to the sparse radar setting, where voxel-based or
Fourier-based approaches would introduce unnecessary complexity.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:moments-preprocessing}

\paragraph{Feature selection.}
Each detected point carries six raw features: $x, y, z$ (Cartesian
coordinates), range $r$, radial velocity $v$, bearing $\theta$, and intensity
$I$. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:radhar}, Cartesian coordinates and polar
features are geometrically redundant. We found it sufficient to work with a
compact five-dimensional per-point representation:
\[
p_i = (x_i,\; y_i,\; z_i,\; v_i,\; I_i) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^5.
\]

\paragraph{Moment extraction.}
For a frame $\mathcal{F}_t = \{p_1, \ldots, p_{N_t}\}$, we compute six
statistics along each of the $d = 5$ feature dimensions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Statistic & Definition \\
\midrule
Mean          & $\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i p_{ik}$ \\[4pt]
Std.\ dev.    & $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i (p_{ik}-\mu_k)^2}$ \\[4pt]
Skewness      & $\gamma_{1,k} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i \left(\frac{p_{ik}-\mu_k}{\sigma_k}\right)^3$ \\[4pt]
Kurtosis      & $\gamma_{2,k} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i \left(\frac{p_{ik}-\mu_k}{\sigma_k}\right)^4 - 3$ \\[4pt]
Minimum       & $\min_i\, p_{ik}$ \\[4pt]
Maximum       & $\max_i\, p_{ik}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Concatenating these statistics across all $d = 5$ dimensions yields a
fixed-size descriptor
\[
s_t \;=\; \bigl[\mu_1,\,\sigma_1,\,\gamma_{1,1},\,\gamma_{2,1},\,
               \min_1,\,\max_1,\;\ldots\;\bigr] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{30},
\]
regardless of the number of points $N_t$ in the frame. This descriptor serves
as the input to the classifier described below.

\paragraph{Temporal aggregation.}
In contrast to the sketching pipeline, which operates over a 2-second window of
60 frames and feeds a $(60, m)$ tensor to a sequential model, the moment
baseline treats each frame \emph{independently}: a single frame is compressed
into one 30-dimensional vector, which is passed directly to an MLP. This
amounts to ignoring the temporal structure of the recording. The implications
for performance and a discussion of how temporal information could be
reintroduced are given in Section~\ref{sec:moments-discussion}.

\paragraph{Standardization.}
The 30-dimensional feature vector is standardized to zero mean and unit
variance using statistics computed on the training set only, following the
same protocol as in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\subsection{Architecture: Multi-Layer Perceptron}

Since the 30-dimensional moment vector has no spatial or sequential structure,
convolutional or recurrent architectures bring no benefit here. We therefore use
a fully-connected MLP with three hidden layers:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Layer   & Output dim & Activation & Note \\
\midrule
Input   & 30         & ---        & Standardized moment vector \\
Dense 1 & 128        & ReLU       & \\
Dense 2 & 64         & ReLU       & \\
Dense 3 & 32         & ReLU       & \\
Output  & 5          & Softmax    & One logit per activity class \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The model is trained with early stopping (patience of 10 epochs,
validation fraction 15\%) to prevent overfitting on the relatively small
dataset. No dropout or weight decay was applied, as the model is already
severely regularized by the low input dimensionality.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:moments-discussion}

Table~\ref{tab:moments-per-class} reports per-class precision, recall and
F1-score on the RadHAR test set (58 samples, 5 classes).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Per-class performance of the moment-based MLP on RadHAR (test set).}
\label{tab:moments-per-class}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Activity & Precision & Recall & F1-score & Support \\
\midrule
Boxing  & 0.52 & 0.85 & 0.65 & 13 \\
Jack    & 0.67 & 0.20 & 0.31 & 10 \\
Jump    & 0.62 & 0.45 & 0.53 & 11 \\
Squats  & 1.00 & 0.20 & 0.33 & 10 \\
Walk    & 0.58 & 1.00 & 0.74 & 14 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Overall accuracy} & \multicolumn{4}{c}{\textbf{58.6\%}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The model achieves \textbf{58.6\%} accuracy, well above the
random baseline of 20\%. Locomotor activities with a clear spatial signature
(\textit{walk}: 100\% recall; \textit{boxing}: 85\% recall) are well captured
by the moment representation. By contrast, activities involving repetitive
vertical motion confined to a small spatial region (\textit{squats},
\textit{jack}) are poorly recalled (20\%), likely because a single-frame
moment descriptor cannot encode the periodicity that distinguishes these
activities from a static standing posture.

This limitation is a direct consequence of the \emph{temporal blindness} of
the current approach. Integrating temporal information --- for instance by
computing moments over a 2-second sliding window rather than per frame, or by
feeding the sequence of per-frame descriptors to an LSTM --- is the most
natural next step and is expected to close a significant part of the gap with
the sequential architectures described in
Section~\ref{sec:sketching-transformer}.
